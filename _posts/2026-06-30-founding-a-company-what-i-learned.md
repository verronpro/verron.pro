In June a colleague in Beijing and I started a partnership. We called it Jing
Joes. The idea was simple: AI has made a whole class of apps and services
feasible that weren't before, and we wanted to build some of them, ideally for
side revenue, and at worst for ourselves.

Our first app is Ember字, an HSK spaced-repetition app. I'll write about it here
eventually. This post is about the other thing: what it takes to turn "let's
build something together" into a company.

## Why proprietary

My partner isn't convinced by open source. His argument is simple: if we want to
sell something, and the whole service with all its balancing under the hood is
public, AI makes it very easy for someone to clone what took us months to tune.
Proprietary is a defence against that, not an ideology. I've stopped arguing.

## The agreement

We were mostly in sync on the big things. The one point that took work was
agreeing from when we start spending our own money on the venture. That's a
harder line than it sounds, because it's where "we'll build something together"
becomes a financial commitment.

The agreement also covers the uncomfortable case: what happens if one of us
stops contributing early. That has to be written down before it happens, not
after, because after is when everyone is angry and no one wants to negotiate.

What we both agree matters most is simpler: stay in touch, so either of us can
ask the other for whatever we need to progress.

## The tooling loss

The surprise was the tooling. I've always built on open source, and I took a lot
of it for granted. On a closed proprietary project, GitHub becomes a paid
service for features I used for free: private everything, more actions, more
seats. I know I can rebuild most of it on my own laptop. That doesn't make it
hurt less. The loss is felt deeply, every time a workflow I used without
thinking now costs something or just isn't there.

## What I'd tell someone starting

- Write the exit rules before you need them. Fair and balanced when one partner
  fades early is the whole game.
- Decide the open-source question early, and make it a decision, not a default.
- Budget for the closed-source tax on tools. It's real and it's recurring.
- A partner who covers your weak side (for me, UI and marketing) is worth more
  than a smarter copy of yourself.
- The company is also there to push you to ship. That part is easy to forget and
  maybe the most valuable part.
